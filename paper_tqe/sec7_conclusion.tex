\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We introduce SiliQun, an open-source simulation platform grounded in quantum physics that integrates silicon spin qubit technologies—including SiMOS quantum dots, phosphorus-donor systems, and gate-all-around nanowires—within a unified Lindblad density-matrix engine compatible with Gymnasium. Developed using the Physics-Grounded Iterative RL Stack (PGIRS) methodology, this approach systematically enforces noise-first design, interface abstraction, reward engineering, hierarchical decomposition, and empirical validation throughout the simulation stack. Validation spans four independent replications across three hardware platforms and three reinforcement learning algorithms, establishing SiliQun as the most rigorously tested open-source silicon spin qubit simulator currently available.

Our experimental findings collectively demonstrate SiliQun's capabilities. The Lindblad physics engine achieves machine-precision validation across analytical benchmarks: Rabi oscillations, $T_1$ amplitude decay (maximum deviation $2.78 \times 10^{-16}$, at float64 epsilon), and Bell state fidelity versus coherence time. Cross-technology consistency emerges through replication studies, where we successfully reproduce Daraeizadeh~et~al.'s DQL/SARSA CZ gate-synthesis experiment \cite{Daraeizadeh2020} on SiMOS ($\Delta F < 0.04$) and He~et~al.'s universal state-preparation protocol \cite{He2021} on donor systems ($\bar{F} = 0.9748 \pm 0.0098$). Applying the same DQN protocol to GAA nanowires yields $\bar{F} = 0.9877 \pm 0.0021$, representing what we believe is the first machine-learning benchmark for this platform. The DRL validation further confirms two complementary aspects: a lightweight PPO agent achieves $F = 0.9996$ on 2-qubit Bell state synthesis, verifying both the physics engine's learnable reward signals and correct Gymnasium implementation, while managing only $F = 0.3998$ on the 4-qubit GHZ task—demonstrating physically realistic noise modeling and genuine task difficulty without specialized enhancements.

Beyond these immediate outcomes, SiliQun's architectural contribution proves particularly significant. Its hardware-neutral simulation substrate enables unprecedented cross-technology comparisons and transfer-learning studies by exposing all silicon spin qubit variants through a standardized Gymnasium interface, overcoming previous tool fragmentation. The PGIRS methodology provides clear engineering principles for extending the platform to emerging qubit technologies like superconducting transmons or neutral atoms without modifying the core Lindblad solver or interface.

Three critical directions emerge for future work. Direct comparisons against classical optimal control methods (GRAPE \cite{Khaneja2005}, CRAB \cite{Caneva2011}) on SiMOS and donor platforms could clarify when reinforcement learning offers practical advantages. Validating hardware profiles against physical devices through randomized benchmarking would help bridge the simulation-to-reality gap currently limiting policy deployment. Finally, extending scalability studies beyond four qubits may reveal whether the observed noise-conditional hyperparameter relationships generalize to larger registers and whether PGIRS can guide simulation design for non-silicon platforms without core modifications.

SiliQun is available under the MIT licence at \url{https://github.com/r3d-phd/siliqun}, with full reproducibility ensured through provided scripts and archived Aziz HPC job files.
